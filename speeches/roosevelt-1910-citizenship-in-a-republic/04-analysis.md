# Citizenship in a Republic — Speechwriting Analysis

## Speech at a Glance

Roosevelt's validated primary purposes are `establish_values` and `persuade`.
The speech defines a demanding standard of democratic citizenship and argues
for that standard through distinctions, examples, and warnings. Its central
concern is not simply that citizens admire virtue, but that they join moral
intention to effective action.

Its secondary purposes are `call_to_action`, `inspire`, `warn`, `unify`, and
`define_stakes`. Directives concerning work, worthy action, righteous defense,
practical reform, reciprocal liberty, and electoral judgment translate the
value framework into conduct. Praise of courage elevates aspiration; warnings
expose the dangers of cynicism, idle wealth, ungoverned ability, fanaticism,
and demagoguery; appeals to judgment by conduct resist class and religious
division; and references to republican survival make the consequences clear.

The validated themes are `civic_duty`, `responsibility`, `freedom`,
`national_unity`, `international_cooperation`, and `sacrifice`. Civic duty and
responsibility organize the whole address. Freedom becomes most explicit in
the defense of minority rights and self-government; national unity is tested
against class hatred and prejudice; international cooperation emerges in the
discussion of law, peaceful settlement, and Franco-American friendship; and
sacrifice appears in the costs of work, service, defense, and action despite
possible failure.

The validated tone combines `formal`, `idealistic`, `challenging`, and
`resolute`. The Sorbonne setting and historical register sustain formality.
Moral aspiration coexists with sharp rebuke, while firm commitments to justice
remain compatible with tolerance, practical compromise, and peaceful
international aims.

The architecture moves from an Old World–New World framing of the occasion to
the dependence of democracy on citizen quality. It then tests citizenship in
successive domains: privileged intellect, character and action, work and
family, wealth and public benefit, ability and moral control, practical reform,
equality, liberty, class division, electoral judgment, and duties among
nations. A final tribute returns the argument to the French hosts.

## Rhetorical Structure

**s01 — The New World at the Sorbonne (p001–p004)**  
Validated functions: `opening`, `reframe_occasion`

Roosevelt begins by placing himself, a speaker from the New World, inside an
ancient European institution. He reconstructs the American movement from
frontier hardship through industrial materialism to a renewed search for
learning and ideals. The occasion therefore becomes an exchange between
civilizations rather than a ceremonial appearance. This historical frame
prepares s02 by explaining why a modern republic should examine the character
needed to direct its material power.

**s02 — Democracy's Dependence on the Citizen (p005–p006)**  
Validated functions: `define_stakes`, `personalize_responsibility`

The speech announces individual citizenship as its subject and distinguishes
republics from systems governed by one person or a few. Under democracy, the
quality of ordinary men and women determines national success, while leaders
must meet an even higher standard. This thesis turns political theory into
personal responsibility and supplies the governing test that every later
domain applies.

**s03 — From Privilege to Action (p007–p009)**  
Validated functions: `address_audience_group`, `establish_values`

Roosevelt addresses educated and privileged listeners directly: greater
advantages entail greater expectations. He identifies cynicism and detached
criticism as characteristic temptations of cultivated people, then answers
them with the image of the person acting in the arena. P009 is the emphatic
completion of the argument begun at p007, not a separate speech or an isolated
lesson. Its standard of imperfect but worthy action becomes a premise for the
more specific civic tests that follow.

**s04 — Character Above Cultivation (p010–p011)**  
Validated function: `establish_values`

France demonstrates that artistic and intellectual distinction can coexist
with courage and leadership. Roosevelt then ranks body, mind, and character,
placing self-restraint, common sense, responsibility, cooperation, courage,
and resolution above cultivation alone. This positive synthesis answers s03's
critique of sterile refinement and introduces the ordinary virtues developed
in s05.

**s05 — Work, Defense, and Family Continuity (p012–p014)**  
Validated functions: `establish_values`, `define_stakes`

Three ordinary obligations organize the section: earning one's livelihood,
being able to fight for righteousness when necessary, and raising a healthy
family. Roosevelt presents each as a condition of individual worth and social
continuity. The movement enlarges the consequences from idleness, through a
nation's ability to resist wrong, to the ability of a civilization to endure
across generations.

**s06 — Wealth as Foundation, Not Final Standard (p015–p018)**  
Validated functions: `establish_values`, `reframe_problem`

Material provision is first affirmed as a duty to self and family, but wealth
is then repositioned as only the foundation of a higher life. Accumulation is
judged by the service returned to the community, and property rights are
protected while remaining subordinate to human rights in a genuine conflict.
The section shifts the test of citizenship from possessing material power to
using it for public benefit.

**s07 — Ability Under Moral Government (p019–p022)**  
Validated functions: `establish_values`, `define_stakes`

Money-making, oratory, journalism, and personal efficiency are treated as
forms of power whose value depends on their direction. Roosevelt refuses both
harmless incapacity and capable wickedness: the citizen must be effective, but
effectiveness without moral sense magnifies danger. This synthesis extends
s06's judgment of wealth into a general rule for talent, influence, and public
leadership.

**s08 — Ideals Made Practical (p023–p025)**  
Validated functions: `establish_values`, `reframe_problem`

The argument moves from household character to political reform. Roosevelt
rejects the doctrinaire, the impossible visionary, and the supposedly
practical person who abandons morality. He presents high ideals and workable
means as mutually necessary, then refuses a fixed boundary between individual
initiative and common action. This prepares s09's more focused account of how
equality and reform should be pursued.

**s09 — Equality of Opportunity and Experimental Reform (p026–p030)**  
Validated functions: `establish_values`, `reframe_problem`, `call_to_action`

Lincoln's account of the Declaration supports a distinction between equal
rights and identical capacities. Roosevelt argues for equality of opportunity,
rewards proportioned to service, assistance for those who stumble, and no
guaranteed equivalence between effort and shirking. The conclusion converts
that framework into method: listeners should inspect proposals on their
merits, try promising measures, retain what works, and refuse both blanket
rejection and automatic progression to extremes.

**s10 — Reciprocal Liberty Against Division (p031–p033)**  
Validated functions: `establish_values`, `define_stakes`

Liberty is tested by whether citizens protect for others what they claim for
themselves, especially across religious, political, social, and economic
differences. Arrogance toward the poor and hatred of the wealthy are presented
as parallel failures. By recalling republics destroyed when class loyalty
displaced civic loyalty, Roosevelt makes tolerance and judgment by conduct
conditions of republican survival rather than matters of politeness.

**s11 — A Voter's Test for the Demagogue (p034–p035)**  
Validated function: `personalize_responsibility`

The warning about divisive leaders becomes concrete through the story of a
cowboy willing to misbrand another person's animal for his employer. The
lesson is then transferred to the listener's vote: a politician willing to do
wrong for a supporter will do wrong against that supporter when advantage
changes. The anecdote turns s10's systemic danger into a usable rule of
individual civic judgment.

**s12 — Citizenship Among Nations (p036–p039)**  
Validated functions: `establish_values`, `explain_problem`, `define_stakes`

Roosevelt expands citizenship from relations with family, neighbor, and state
to the duties states owe one another. Patriotism is presented as compatible
with service to humanity, and the morality governing private promises is
extended to political and national conduct. Yet international law lacks the
external sanction of municipal law. The resulting duty is double: statesmen
should strengthen alternatives to force, while nations retain the will and
capacity to resist injustice.

**s13 — A Republican Farewell to France (p040)**  
Validated functions: `address_audience_group`, `closing`

The parting address returns explicitly to the French hosts. Roosevelt links
the two republics through friendship and shared consequence, then praises
France's brilliance, gallantry, historical resilience, and future role. The
closing resolves the Old World–New World relationship introduced in s01 and
ends with confidence rather than another rule or warning.

## Rhetorical Progression

The speech begins with place and history rather than immediate prescription.
By contrasting Europe's accumulated learning with America's frontier and
industrial development, Roosevelt creates a reason for the New World to speak
and to listen. Material success has produced the need for moral and
intellectual direction. That frame makes citizenship the answer to a problem
already established by the opening.

S02 supplies the controlling proposition: in a republic the average citizen
is not politically negligible. Because national greatness cannot rise above
its civic source, character is a public cause rather than a private ornament.
The speech then narrows first to the audience members most likely to mistake
education or privilege for sufficient merit. S03 and s04 oppose detached
criticism to action and subordinate cultivation to character without rejecting
learning itself.

The middle of the address proceeds by testing that character in widening areas
of conduct. Work, military courage, and family obligation concern basic social
continuity. Material provision then leads to wealth, and wealth leads to the
larger question of how any capacity should be judged. Orators and journalists
join financiers as examples because all possess powers capable of public good
or public corruption. By s07, the initial praise of action has acquired a
qualification: action and efficiency count only when morally directed.

S08 and s09 translate the character argument into a theory of reform. High
ideals must survive contact with practical conditions, but practicality cannot
become an excuse for moral indifference. Likewise, equality is neither denied
nor made synonymous with identical ability and reward. The movement from
principle to experiment is essential: the listener is taught not merely what
to value but how to judge proposals amid competing labels.

The political danger then shifts from intellectual rigidity to social
division. Reciprocal liberty, minority treatment, and tolerance provide the
positive standard; class hatred, fanaticism, and leaders who offer private gain
at others' expense provide the threat. The ranch anecdote interrupts the
abstract register with a compact narrative and gives the voter a memorable
test for corrupt inducement.

Finally, the same moral logic crosses national borders. The scale changes, but
the basic demand does not: strength must be governed by right, promises must
retain moral force, and peaceful institutions should be built without making
justice dependent on weakness. The farewell to France then returns from
general international principle to the actual audience and completes the
speech's geographical frame.

## Rhetorical Devices

- `direct_address` — p005, “you and we are citizens of great democratic
  republics”: Roosevelt joins speaker and audience inside the same political
  experiment just as he announces the subject of citizenship. The shared
  pronouns keep the coming judgments from sounding applicable only to America.

- `antithesis` — p005, “success ... glory” against “failure ... despair”: The
  balanced alternatives compress the stakes of republican citizenship into a
  memorable opposition at the moment the central thesis is introduced.

- `metaphor` — p006, “The stream will not permanently rise higher than the main
  source”: The image makes the quality of citizenship causally prior to
  national greatness. Its placement turns an abstract theory of democracy into
  the physical logic that drives the rest of the speech.

- `metaphor` — p009, “the man who is actually in the arena”: Public effort is
  imagined as exposed physical struggle. The image intensifies s03's rejection
  of detached criticism, but its role remains local: it establishes the action
  ethic that later sections qualify and apply.

- `tricolon` — p012, “to work, to fight at need, and to have plenty of healthy
  children”: The three coordinated phrases announce the sequence of ordinary
  duties treated in s05 and give a long argument an audible local map.

- `rhetorical_question` — p013, “Is the right to prevail? Are the great laws of
  righteousness once more to be fulfilled?”: The questions replace a simple
  peace-versus-war choice with a moral test and prepare the explicit answer
  “Yes.” Their placement forces the audience to judge conflict by justice.

- `anaphora` — p014, “No refinement ... no delicacy ... no material progress
  ... no sordid heaping up of riches”: Repeated negatives deny a succession of
  possible substitutes for social continuity, escalating the section's warning.

- `metaphor` — p015–p016, “the solid material foundation” and “the
  superstructure of a higher life”: The extended building image both affirms
  economic provision and limits it. Material welfare supports moral life but
  cannot serve as its final measure.

- `antithesis` — p018, “property belongs to man and not man to property”: The
  grammatical reversal crystallizes the section's hierarchy of human and
  property rights after the longer discussion of wealth and service.

- `allusion` — p026–p027, the appeal to Abraham Lincoln's interpretation of the
  Declaration: Lincoln and the founding text lend historical and moral
  authority to Roosevelt's distinction between equal rights and equal
  capacities.

- `imperative` — p030, “look at it on its merits” and “disregard formulas”:
  Direct commands convert the theory of practical reform into a method the
  audience can use when confronting actual proposals.

- `anaphora` — p037, three sentences beginning “I do not for one moment
  admit”: The repeated opening gives firmness and continuity to the claim that
  private, political, and international morality cannot be separated.

- `parallelism` — p039, “It is the duty of wise statesmen” and “It is the duty
  of every honest statesman”: Matching structures coordinate the positive work
  of building alternatives to force with the restraining duty not to wrong
  other nations.

## Reusable Writing Patterns

- `reframe_occasion` — p001–p004: Roosevelt turns a visit to the Sorbonne into
  an encounter between the accumulated learning of the Old World and the
  energetic but materially absorbed New World. **Cautious principle:** Use the
  venue's history to establish why the subject matters to both speaker and
  audience, not merely to decorate the opening.

- `narrative_reconstruction` — p002–p004: Frontier struggle gives way to towns,
  industrial materialism, and a renewed search for culture. **Cautious
  principle:** A compressed historical sequence can explain why a present
  audience needs a particular argument before that argument is announced.

- `establish_shared_values` — p005–p006: French and American listeners are
  joined as citizens of democratic republics whose success depends on civic
  quality. **Cautious principle:** Shared political conditions can establish
  common ground without pretending that speaker and audience have identical
  histories.

- `raise_stakes` — p005–p006: The conduct of the average person grows into a
  question of national greatness and humanity's verdict on republican
  government. **Cautious principle:** Show the causal route from ordinary
  behavior to collective consequence instead of merely declaring an issue
  important.

- `transfer_responsibility_to_audience` — p007: “Much has been given” becomes
  “much should be expected” specifically from the educated listeners.
  **Cautious principle:** When addressing a privileged audience, connect its
  advantages to a particular burden rather than relying on praise alone.

- `contrast_alternatives` — p023–p025: Morality-free practicality and
  unrealizable idealism are presented as opposing failures, with practical
  moral action between them. **Cautious principle:** A useful middle course is
  clearer when both distortions are developed fairly enough to reveal what the
  alternative must preserve from each.

- `narrative_reconstruction` — p034: The maverick story moves from discovery to
  attempted theft, dismissal, and explanation. **Cautious principle:** A short
  causal narrative can turn an abstract warning into a rule of judgment that
  listeners can remember and transfer to another domain.

- `personalize_responsibility` — p035: “your vote” translates the cowboy story
  into the listener's encounter with a self-serving politician. **Cautious
  principle:** After an analogy or anecdote, state how its logic changes the
  audience's own decision.

- `contrast_alternatives` — p038–p039: Building peaceful means of settlement is
  distinguished from abandoning the power to resist wrong. **Cautious
  principle:** When two desired values can conflict, acknowledge the tension
  and define the limiting condition rather than dissolving it rhetorically.

## Speech-Specific Analysis

### The Citizen as the Source of the Republic

Roosevelt's core democratic claim is causal. In s02, the average citizen is
not simply a beneficiary of institutions; citizen quality is the “main source”
of national power. That premise explains why the speech can move among conduct
that modern audiences might separate into private, economic, political, and
international categories. Work, family duty, use of wealth, response to
demagogues, and national honor all feed the same republican system.

The argument also creates unequal expectations without abandoning democratic
equality. The average citizen must meet a sound standard, but leaders and the
educated must meet a higher one because their advantages and influence are
greater. Responsibility is proportional to capacity. That principle recurs
when wealth, oratory, journalism, and political leadership are judged by their
public effects.

This is why the speech's `civic_duty` and `responsibility` themes cannot be
reduced to the exhortation at p009. The arena image states that action is
better than sterile criticism; the remaining speech asks what kind of action,
under what moral control, toward which people, and with what consequences for
the republic.

### Energy Is Necessary but Not Sufficient

The famous contrast between critic and actor might seem to make energetic
effort the final standard. S07 explicitly prevents that reading. Roosevelt
first insists that harmless inefficiency is not virtue, then insists with equal
force that greater efficiency makes an evil person more dangerous. The
movement from p009 to p021–p022 revises no earlier principle; it completes it.
Citizens need the capacity to act and the moral sense to govern action.

The same two-part test organizes several domains. Wealth may demonstrate
ability, but it deserves credit only when earned or used beneficially. Oratory
may communicate conviction, but it becomes harmful when it gives false values
persuasive force. Journalism may serve public understanding, but its reach
magnifies mendacity and sensationalism. Courage can defend righteousness, but
force is not made righteous merely by being vigorous.

This recurring structure supports the speech's `challenging` tone. Roosevelt
denies comfortable moral identities to both the passive good person and the
successful wrongdoer. Listeners cannot claim citizenship through refinement,
good intentions, wealth, eloquence, or effectiveness alone.

### Reform Between Formula and Drift

S08 and s09 offer a method for political judgment rather than a fixed program.
Roosevelt rejects extreme individualism and extreme socialism as doctrinaire
positions, yet allows that common action may solve problems individual action
cannot. The drainage and water-supply example makes scale and changed
conditions, not ideological names, the relevant criteria.

His account of equality uses a similar distinction. Lincoln's words authorize
equal inalienable rights as a continuing standard, while Roosevelt separates
that standard from a claim that people possess identical capacities or should
receive identical rewards. “Level up” preserves opportunity and assistance;
the warning against “leveling down” preserves effort and service as bases for
reward.

P030 supplies the practical consequence. Proposals are to be examined, tried,
accepted when successful, and rejected when not. Partial cooperation does not
require agreement about a distant tenth step. In ordinary analytical terms,
the passage makes room for accommodation without weakening its practical test;
no accommodating tone is separately validated in the canonical tags.

### From Reciprocal Liberty to International Duty

The discussion of liberty adds reciprocity to the earlier action ethic. A good
citizen must secure for others the freedom claimed for the self, and minority
treatment becomes the practical test. Roosevelt applies the same symmetry to
class conflict: contempt directed downward and hatred directed upward are
different manifestations of the same civic failure. This supports the
`national_unity` theme by locating the relevant division between right and
wrong conduct rather than between economic or occupational groups.

The ranch anecdote then makes corrupt reciprocity visible. The cowboy believes
that stealing for his employer demonstrates loyalty; Roosevelt answers that a
person willing to violate a principle for one patron will violate it against
that patron. P035 applies the same reasoning to politicians who promise
wrongful private advantage. Civic loyalty is therefore tested by refusal of
benefits that depend on injury to fellow citizens.

S12 carries the logic beyond the state. Patriotism need not erase concern for
humanity, and national promises remain subject to ordinary morality. Yet the
analogy has a limit: international society lacks an all-powerful external
sanction. Roosevelt therefore joins the `international_cooperation` theme to a
resolute defense of justice. States should build alternatives to force, but
peace is not treated as permission to submit to wrong.

## Climax and Closing

No section is validated with the canonical function `climax`. The speech is
organized less as a steady ascent toward one high point than as a cumulative
series of tests applied to citizenship. Its strongest emphases are distributed:
p009 concentrates the case for action, p018 reverses the relation between
people and property, p032 warns that class division can destroy republics, and
p039 joins peace to justice in international life.

P009 is especially memorable, but neither fame nor intensity makes it the
canonical climax of this structure. It arrives in s03, before the substantial
arguments about ordinary duty, wealth, moral efficiency, reform, equality,
liberty, demagoguery, and international responsibility. Analytically, it is an
early statement of energy and commitment whose meaning is disciplined by the
later insistence that effective action must serve moral and public ends.

The validated `closing` is s13, p040. It does not summarize every civic rule or
issue one final command. Instead, Roosevelt returns to “my hosts,” renews the
bond between the American and French republics, and presents France's
historical resilience as grounds for confidence in its future. The ending
completes the address by returning to the relationship and civilizational frame
with which it began.

## Speechwriting Blueprint

This sequence is derived from Roosevelt's speech and is not a universal
formula:

1. Use the venue and its history to create a shared reason for the subject.
2. State a governing thesis that links ordinary conduct to collective outcomes.
3. Place heightened responsibility on listeners with heightened advantages.
4. Contrast detached judgment with imperfect but worthy action.
5. Define the character that should govern action.
6. Test that character in concrete personal and social duties.
7. Judge wealth, talent, and influence by the public ends they serve.
8. Balance moral aspiration with practical means and experimental reform.
9. Clarify equality by distinguishing rights, opportunity, capacity, and reward.
10. Define liberty reciprocally and expose the danger of internal division.
11. Use a personal story to give voters a memorable test for corruption.
12. Extend domestic moral principles to relations among nations while
    acknowledging institutional differences.
13. Close by returning to the hosts and the relationship established at the
    opening.

## Questions for Study

1. How does the Old World–New World contrast in s01 establish a need for the
   speech's argument about citizenship?
2. What does the stream-and-source `metaphor` in p006 imply about democratic
   institutions and ordinary conduct?
3. Why does Roosevelt address educated and privileged listeners before the
   arena passage?
4. How do p010–p011 prevent the critique of refinement from becoming a critique
   of learning itself?
5. What progression links work, military courage, and family continuity in
   s05?
6. How does the foundation-and-superstructure `metaphor` let Roosevelt affirm
   material welfare while denying that wealth is a final standard?
7. Why are money-making, oratory, journalism, and efficiency analyzed in the
   same part of the speech?
8. How do s08 and s09 distinguish practical reform from both ideological
   rigidity and moral compromise?
9. What authority does the Lincoln `allusion` lend to Roosevelt's definition of
   equality, and where does Roosevelt add his own distinctions?
10. How does reciprocal liberty lead into the warning about class division?
11. Why might the ranch anecdote be more memorable than an abstract warning
    against political self-interest?
12. Where does the speech's `challenging` tone coexist with its practical
    openness to tolerance, cooperation, and negotiated settlement?
13. What remains constant, and what changes, when Roosevelt moves from private
    and civic morality to international relations?
14. Why is p009 best understood as part of s03 rather than as a standalone
    speech or the climax of the whole address?
15. How does p040 complete the frame established in p001–p004 without
    summarizing the entire argument?

## Taxonomy Notes

The canonical distinction between the primary purposes `establish_values` and
`persuade` and the secondary purpose `call_to_action` is particularly useful
here. Roosevelt does not merely praise character: he defines its elements,
argues through contested applications, and also directs conduct without making
those directives the address's dominant purpose. The validated tones
`challenging` and `resolute` capture its exacting demands and firmness. Its
openness to tolerance, partial cooperation, experimental reform, and peaceful
settlement remains an ordinary analytical observation rather than an
additional canonical tone classification.

The distinction between the section function `personalize_responsibility` and
the writing patterns `transfer_responsibility_to_audience` and
`personalize_responsibility` clarifies two different movements. In s03, general
responsibility is shifted toward the educated audience because of its
advantages; in s11, an abstract warning becomes the individual listener's rule
for judging a vote.

No canonical `climax` was validated, and the analysis preserves that absence.
The arena `metaphor` is a canonical device annotation, not evidence by itself
for a whole-speech climax or a separate speech unit.

Several major subjects exceed the current canonical theme vocabulary. Character,
work, family, wealth, equality of opportunity, tolerance, and patriotism are
substantively important here, but they remain ordinary, noncanonical analytical
descriptions in this document. Their absence from the validated theme list does
not make them absent from the speech, and this observation does not create or
approve taxonomy candidates.
